# Chapter 3: Calling the Debt

The man who owed Aunt Reet sixty kroons worked on the Salt Quay, and on the morning of the third day Teele was going to read his debt aloud in front of everyone he worked with.

"You don't have to come," she said. "I've done forty of these."

"Forty without a man behind you who can borrow a skill," said Aarne. "I'd like to be standing there with one that's less than an hour old."

So he stood against a bollard with his hat low and watched Nurm work. Nurm was a bale-man, taller than the door of the pawnshop, with a leather harness across his shoulders and four cuts, which the machine had spent on a skill called Bale-Heft. It let him pick up a forty-kilo sack of river salt as if it were a loaf. He took one in each hand, ran them up a plank into the barge hold, and came down for more, and by the time Aarne had watched him do it for ten minutes the stick, inside his coat, had written the offer without being asked.

*Loan available: Bale-Heft, four cuts, lent by Pärn, Nurm. At your strength: one hundred grams for each second the skill is doing something.*

A hundred a second. In the pawnshop he had paid for six seconds of whistling and gone dizzy. Ten seconds of lifting would be a kilo. He put the number away where he kept the other ones, and the stick went quiet.

Along the quay the barley women had their pots going, and two of them were talking over the steam. "Bell rang again last night," said one. "Twice."

"It rings twice every winter."

"It rang twice and then once more, and the guards went out with the salt carts."

"They go out every year."

The Salt Quay was a long stone wall with barges tied along it, and on the far side of the channel the evaporation pans lay flat and white as far as the eye went, with a lookout hut at each corner. Out on the white, a long way off, something was walking. It was the grey-white of the pans themselves and the height of a man, with arms that hung straight and a head but no face Aarne could make out, and it went in a slow line toward the channel. Two guards ran out from the nearest hut with a cart of salt, and then the glare off the pans swallowed it. Nobody else on the quay looked up. That was a rime, Aarne knew from the pamphlet nobody read. A rime was what grew on a salt pan when a debtor died out there still owing, a standing crust the shape of a person that walked home at the first hard frost to collect. The pan guards could usually salt one back down before it reached the lamps, though not every time.

Teele climbed on a stack of crates and filled her chest.

"*Hear it,*" she said, in her rat-calling voice. "*Nurm Pärn, bale-man, owes Reet Rõuk, keeper of the Salt Quarter pawnshop, sixty kroons on a loan of the nineteenth of Raking, against a pair of silver buckles, and has not paid it, nor said why.*"

Calling the debt was the oldest weapon in the Quarter. You read the obligation aloud where the man worked, slowly, with his name and the sum, and nobody likes to be the third man in the queue behind the one who doesn't pay. The barge-boys moved their lips with the number. A barley woman stopped stirring.

Nurm put both sacks down, carefully. "That's Reet's niece," he said to the quay. "That's a child with a mouth."

"*And has not paid it, nor said why,*" said Teele, who had memorised the sentence and could not skip any part of it.

He crossed the quay in four strides, shut a fist on the back of her coat and lifted her until her boots left the crate. With his other hand he reached down for the nearest sack. "I'm going to put you in the harbour," he said, "and then I'm going to put this on top of you."

Aarne had meant to wait for him to put her down. He saw Nurm's arm go back, and the sack with it, and decided that the time for watching was over.

"I accept," he said.

The skill came up through his arms like a wave through a rope. He had never lifted anything heavier than a pie crate, and he knew without trying that he could now pick up the barge. Nurm, with a sack on its way to his shoulder and a girl in his other fist, felt his arm go slack.

"What," said Nurm. The sack slid off his shoulder and landed on his own foot.

He screamed and let go of Teele, who landed on her boots and said, "That's the second time I've been owed damages." The sack split on the planks and spilled salt in a long white skid across the quay. The salt scattered a stack of eighteen more sacks that had been piled beside the plank, and the stack began, very slowly, to lean over a barge-boy who was sitting against it eating bread.

Aarne counted. He was three seconds in. He walked across the quay, took the burst sack off Nurm's foot with one hand, and set it aside, and then he put his shoulder against the stack and held it. It weighed about as much as a cart. He felt it in his arms the way you feel a handrail, present and not heavy. The barge-boy got up from his bread and ran.

At seven seconds the barley women had dragged Teele out of the way. At ten the stack stood upright again on a clear patch of quay, and Aarne took his shoulder off it and said "Return" in an ordinary voice, and felt the skill leave. His knees began to shake and he put a hand on the wall.

Nurm sat on the planks with his foot in both hands and looked at him. "You took it," he said. "You took my lift."

"I gave it back. You'll find it works."

"What did you do that for? Before the sack?"

"You had your foot under one," said Aarne. "I wasn't going to leave a man pinned on the quay."

It took Nurm a long time to get his boot off. When he did, there were sixty-four kroons in it, rolled flat, damp and still warm, and he held them out to Teele without looking at her.

"Sixty for Reet," said Teele, counting. "Four for me. Thank you, Master Pärn. I'll tell my aunt you were very polite."

"You could have kept it," she said afterwards, as they walked off the quay with her coat on crooked. "His lift. You'd have had it all week."

"A bale-man with no lift can't load a barge. And I'd be paying a hundred grams a second every time I used it." His hands were still shaking, and he put them in his pockets, where the stick was. "I wanted to see what a four-cut skill costs. It costs a kilo for ten seconds. I can do that about ten times in total, and then I can't do anything at all."

"That's not very many."

"No."

The pie cart's dough scale was a small iron one with a pan and a set of weights. By evening Aarne had stopped pretending not to look at it. He read the beam as if the cart might not notice, while Liis slept on a flour sack in the lee of the brazier.

Fifty thousand two hundred and ninety. Two kilos less than at the Cutting. He had eaten four pies since breakfast and could feel every one of the missing grams in his hands, which were cold and had a tremor in the fingers.

The Reckoner arrived after dark, with a boy carrying a lantern and a leather case. He stopped at the cart and gave Hilja a short bow. "Madam Kallas. I am Reckoner Lind. May I sit?"

"There's a crate," said Hilja.

He sat on the crate. The boy held the lantern at an angle that made his own face look guilty. Hilja put a pie on a square of paper and handed it over without a word, and Lind, who had so clearly come to say something exact, looked at it and ate it.

"It's cold in the middle," he said.

"It always is."

"I find I don't mind." He wiped his fingers on a handkerchief. "Master Kallas. A complaint was lodged at noon today. Ordinance nine: unlicensed lending. A foreman on the Salt Quay says that you took a man's skill in front of forty witnesses and gave it back after. I have been instructed to suspend your Pending tonight. Suspended, the cost of cutting falls due at once, four hundred kroons, and a fine of two hundred for the lending."

Aarne felt his mother stop folding dough.

"I have the instruction here," said Lind. He did not take it out. "Do you have your receipt?"

"Liis," said Hilja.

Liis, who was apparently not asleep, sat up with the receipt flat in her hand. It had a crease down one edge from the way she had slept on it. "I only folded it a bit," she said.

Aarne took it, and read it by the lantern, aloud, and heard the sentence for the first time as a clerk would. "*Held Pending under Ordinance 14 until the end of the seventh day, to be altered by no one but a bench.* Ordinance nine says a Pending can be suspended on complaint. It doesn't say by whom. This says only a bench can alter it. A bench sits on the fifth day. You can't suspend it tonight."

Lind took the receipt, held it to the lantern for the seal, and gave it back. "I wrote that clause in a hurry," he said. "I do not generally write clauses in a hurry. I am obliged to report to the Notchery that the receipt is in order, and I am not obliged to tell them who drafted it." He pushed his pen-case across the crate toward Liis. "For receipts, young lady. Mind the nib."

Liis took it as if it were a medal.

"The Salt Factors' House has asked for your file," Lind said, getting up. "Madam Oja does not ask for things. She files for them. The bench is on the fifth day, and I would not be alone at it." He went. The boy with the lantern followed and tripped on the Gate step.

"Take your belt off," said Hilja.

"Mam."

"Take it off, Aarne."

He took it off. She held it to the lamp and found the hole he had used since the winter began, and the one beside it that he had started on after the Cutting. She had stitched that belt herself. She took the awl from her apron and made a third, with a flat firm push of the wrist, and put the belt back in his hands.

"Whatever it costs," she said, "you tell me what it costs."

"A kilo for the whistle. A kilo for the quay. I've ten left."

"Ten what?"

"Kilos. Before I can't borrow any more." He had not meant to say it. She looked at him the way she looked at a ledger somebody else had written.

"Then we will not spend them on a quay," said Hilja, and went on working.

He went round the back of the cart to be alone and took the stick out. Under the day's line a new one had been written, in a slightly different slant, as if someone else had held the pencil.
