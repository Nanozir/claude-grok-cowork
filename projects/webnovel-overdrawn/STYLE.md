## PART 1. STYLE CHECKLIST (from "Literature Research")

Section tags in brackets are the doc's headings. Doc's core principle [How to use]: **density, not single tells**. One em dash or one "not X but Y" proves nothing; a cluster in a short passage does (doc cites 21 tells in 451 words). Reviewer method: COUNT, do not go by impression. Test structure, not word lists.

Process note [When Claude helps]: the author writes the messy first draft; the assistant prunes, tightens and critiques, and does NOT rewrite wholesale ("text that an AI rewrote is no longer the author's words"). Feedback should be honest and not padded with praise. For a *new original story* the checklist below is the bar the draft must meet.

### A. Word choice [Never do: word choice]
1. Avoid AI vocabulary: tapestry, testament, delve, intricate, pivotal, crucial, vital, underscore, resonate, robust, nuanced, journey, navigate, landscape, symphony, beacon, foster, showcase, harness, unveil, meticulous, vibrant, realm (unless literal), "a dance of", "an ode to". Fine occasionally; the tell is frequency / wrong context. (Notes section adds: unwavering, formidable, enigmatic, "a testament to".)
2. No "magic" adverbs/adjectives that inflate the ordinary: quietly, softly, deeply, impossibly, remarkably, "something like", "the particular quality of", "the kind of X that".
3. No stock body language: jaw worked/tightened, breath hitched, "breath she didn't know she was holding", eyes darkened, a muscle ticked, smirked (max once a book), "throat" instead of neck, "palms" instead of hands.
4. Dialogue tags: use "said" or an action beat, often no tag. No murmured/breathed/exhaled/rasped/offered/intoned.
5. Use plain "is"/"has", not "serves as / stands as / boasts".
6. No synonym cycling (blade/weapon/steel for one sword). Repeat the word or use a pronoun.
7. No weight words for emotion ("the words carried weight", "that landed", "it cost him something", "earned").
8. No stock romance/reaction clichés: warm inviting smile, flutter in chest, heat in cheeks, eyes twinkling/dancing with mischief.
9. No unnecessary scare quotes.
10. No ghosts/hums/spectral silence: silence should not hum, stretch, wrap or turn loud; use silence rarely, as plain absence of sound.
11. Pet phrases: catch any word repeated within a few pages (the doc's example: a pulled novel used "sharp" 159 times).

### B. Sentence patterns [Never do: sentence patterns]
12. Negative parallelism ("It wasn't fear. It was something worse." / "Not X. Not Y. Just Z."): max ONE per chapter, only if the contrast is real. Called the most recognised tell.
13. No colon-and-fragment twist ("She smiled at him: not with kindness. With liberation.").
14. No back-to-back tricolons; vary counts (one, two, four). No synonym threes ("elegant, refined and sophisticated"). Threes only if each item adds information.
15. No reflective gerund openers as transitions ("Watching him leave, she realized...").
16. No anaphora abuse (several sentences starting the same way) and no echoed structure across paragraphs ("He could... He could...").
17. No self-posed question answered at once ("The cost? Everything.").
18. No comma-clipped tails that trail off ("He left, the door clicking shut behind him, the silence heavy.").
19. No "did not X, which would have been easier" reframes.
20. Do not stack clean pivot sentences ("Julian looked away too late.") One is fine; five in a short passage is the tell.
21. No list-rhythm stacking of specific-sounding items for emotional impact, several times per page.

### C. Rhythm and paragraphs [Never do: rhythm]
22. Uneven, jagged prose: mix long winding sentences with blunt ones; let some paragraphs be messy.
23. One-sentence paragraphs only when the beat truly stands alone, not as drumbeats.
24. Em dashes: a few per chapter, not per paragraph. Use commas, parentheses, full stops, semicolons.
25. Hard cuts between scenes are allowed; no frictionless transitions everywhere.
26. Read aloud / one-breath test [Always do + log]: if three sentences read with no change of pitch and no stumble, it may be over-smoothed. Put some mess back.

### D. Emotion and interiority [Never do: emotion]
27. No emotional labelling as "showing" ("a wave of grief washed over her"). Name feelings rarely; render through action, choice, physical reaction, concrete detail.
28. No wisdom-summary sentence at scene end ("Some wounds never fully closed"). Trust the scene.
29. Do not over-explain motivation or theme; let the reader infer. End scenes a beat early.
30. No hedged shallow interiority ("he found himself wondering", "something in her shifted") and no stacked pseudo-wise emotional shorthand.
31. Not every beat at maximum weight. Let small moments be small, funny or mundane. Vary emotional pitch across scenes (restraint, escalation, reversal, release); a quiet breakfast and a breakup must not share the same heat.

### E. Description [Never do: description]
32. No sensory cataloguing (smell, sound, sight, texture in sequence). Pick the 1-2 senses the POV character would notice now.
33. Specific over generic: details must belong to THIS world/person, not interchangeable ("old parchment and ozone"). Choose diagnostic details. One odd, true detail beats three pretty ones.
34. No forced/empty/circular similes or pseudo-profound pairings ("the silence tasted of regret"); no metaphor stacking/personification (building breathes, heater coughs); no "coated prose" (adjective on every noun, simile on every action).
35. No vague placeless settings: name the place, time, real-world or in-world reference.
36. Do not over-describe things the eye does not need at that moment. Track who is where (no "liminal fog").
37. Action must be filtered through the POV's body, fear and decisions, not a camera list of things breaking.
38. State a trait/look once; later mentions must add something (no descriptor recycling). (Opposite risk noted in log: descriptors that appear once and never return.)
39. Dialogue needs a ground: place and physical business during long talk scenes.

### F. Plot, structure, characters [Never do: plot...]
40. Not tidy, single-track, linear: use subplots, non-linear time, moral ambiguity. Do not resolve tension too fast; leave threads/discomfort; not everything goes the protagonist's way.
41. Protagonists must want things and make choices that cost them (no passive protagonists). Real stakes; some losses stay lost.
42. Each scene must change something (a choice, relationship, knowledge). No "illusion of progress" (same realization repeated).
43. Continuity: keep a running fact list (injuries and which side, time of day, who knows what, positions).
44. No stock archetypes unless subverted (motherly woman, sardonic mentor, mysterious stranger with silver eyes). No generic institutions ("The Council", "The Order", "Northern Reaches") unless the world gives them texture.
45. No dream sequences or dreamlike filler that does no work.
46. Dialogue must have subtext: people dodge, interrupt, misunderstand, change subject, lie. No characters stating exactly what they feel; no explaining what both already know.
47. Dramatise conversations; do not summarise them ("They talked about their lives...") - cited as the strongest AI signal.
48. No staged philosophical debates; let characters argue about something petty and concrete and leave the point unstated.
49. No forced symbolism: motifs need real logic linking them to the character.
50. No abrupt unanchored beats (referring to an event never shown).
51. Not on-the-nose premise: do not put the big element in line one playing exactly as labelled; keep it off-stage for a while, put the real conflict between people.
52. No unearned final escalation/twist; endings must pay off something already planted. Open endings beat bows.

### G. Names [Never do: names]
53. Avoid over-used AI names: Elara, Lyra, Aria, Kael, Mira, Maren, Mara, Zephyr, Evelyn, Voss, Calder, Holloway, Chen, Marcus, Ethan, Liam, Noah, -wick surnames, Eldenmoor, Ironhaven, Stormwatch. Weak signal; the doc exempts the author's own existing characters, but for a NEW story invent names grounded in the world's own languages and cultures (doc suggests e.g. Japanese, Russian, invented tongues). (Flag: several "banned" names, Aria, Mira, Elisabeth, Kael-like, already appear in the author's own work, see Part 2.)

### H. Always do [Always do]
54. Voice first: each POV sounds like that person (vocabulary, jokes, noticing) in the NARRATION, not just dialogue.
55. Let readers work: imply, cut explanation.
56. Plain words for plain things; repeat the right word.
57. Uneven texture; humour beside horror.
58. Earn every big moment; use dramatic constructions only where the story has built to them.
59. Self-check after every draft: count negative parallelisms, tricolons, em dashes, emotion labels, wisdom summaries, sensory lists, AI-vocabulary words. Several per page = rewrite.
60. Opinions AND hedges: POV characters judge things (best/worst) and hedge ("maybe", "as far as I know").
61. Human texture: odd specific coinages, semicolons/parentheses for pacing, real-world and in-world references, playful banter, an occasional rule-breaking move (narrator addressing reader).
62. Backstory goes in quiet reaction beats after a setback, carried by one concrete object (Butcher/Dresden car example).
63. Pair loud with plain: flamboyant character against a grounded one; keep reader unsure who survives (Abercrombie).
64. Set a warm/mundane baseline so setbacks land; let the world breathe between plot beats (pirateaba).
65. Keep conversations moving: each line pulls against or builds on the last; jokes escalate; people talk about one thing while thinking about another; change tactics mid-argument; banter shows trust (different joking with trusted people); snark is defence while earnest about what matters.
66. Leave about a quarter of each character unsaid (outline ~3/4; Wildbow).

### I. Pacing and chapter structure (serial-specific rules scattered through genre notes)
67. End scenes on a setback; follow with a quieter reaction beat (Dresden method).
68. Leave a problem unresolved in the chapter where it appears so tension carries over (Expanse).
69. Plan rests; cut length. Constant escalation exhausts readers (Wildbow/Pact; Worm complaints about long fights/conversations).
70. Take away a character's strengths in a scene; if you cannot find the way out, they cannot either and they pay.
71. Start from "boring" or C-list powers/premises and twist them.
72. Power systems should create problems as often as they solve them; keep rules consistent once stated; wins come from outthinking rules; ration system messages (no status-screen dumps unless funny).
73. Violence unglamorous with lasting costs; every faction believes it is right; comedy and horror can share a scene.
74. Scale through ground-level POVs; power through consequences, not stat comparisons; faction politics concrete; drop exact numbers when they make prose too technical.
75. Culture delivery: short in-universe documents (forms, propaganda, broadcasts) can open chapters; outsider POV learns world at reader's pace; keep a names file and one "sound palette" per culture; lore explained only when it hits characters.
76. POV: close third with head-hopping only at chapter breaks (Stiefvater); first person is used in the author's work. Mood and setting first, then characters; cut characters who complicate the theme.
77. Serial note: some chapters can be slow everyday problems; unusual protagonist roles (innkeeper) take the story out of combat.

### J. Review / critique procedure [When Claude helps ...]
78. Critic loop ("gauntlet loop"): for a key scene, a fresh-context critic compares the draft blind (A/B random order) against a benchmark passage, names the single biggest gap with quoted lines; writer fixes; repeat. Judge parts (opening, one scene, dialogue, transitions) not "the chapter". Critic flags gaps and checks the Never-do lists plus counts; does not ghostwrite.
79. Ask what the author is reaching for before calling something a flaw (slow chapter may be setup).
80. Do not flag bad prose as AI on badness alone; clumsy human writing is still human.
81. Blurbs/ad copy produced with AI are also AI prose: run them against the Never-do lists.

### Formatting
- The doc contains NO explicit rules on tense, POV choice, chapter length, word count, or typography. Only inferences: close third (Stiefvater), "said"-tags, em dashes sparingly, one-sentence paragraphs sparingly. See flags.

### Genre benchmarks named in the doc (useful for tone targeting)
- Comedic isekai/progression: Dungeon Crawler Carl, Wandering Inn, Mother of Learning, Re:Zero, Mushoku Tensei.
- Dark fantasy: The First Law, Prince of Thorns, Berserk, The Black Company (terse reportage, gallows humour, big events in the gap between paragraphs).
- Military/space opera: The Expanse, Red Rising, Hyperion, A Memory Called Empire.
- Urban fantasy/supernatural action: Worm, Dresden Files, Raven Cycle, Jujutsu Kaisen, Chainsaw Man, Steelheart.
- School/powers: My Hero Academia, Mob Psycho 100.

### Ambiguities / flags
- A. No stated tense or POV rule. Author's own texts: Guide for Heroes and In His Shadow = first person past; Rulebreakers and Monolith = third person past. Choose per story; keep consistent.
- B. "Em dashes: a few per chapter" and "negative parallelism: one per chapter" give numbers; "one-sentence paragraphs" and "tricolons" do not. A reviewer must pick thresholds.
- C. The doc says count density but gives no per-page threshold beyond "several in one page means rewrite".
- D. Names rule (53) is a weak signal and the author's own characters break it; do not treat it as hard.
- E. Doc is both a style guide for the author's EXISTING worlds and a general AI-tell list; genre notes (e.g. "Balance and Bypass rules", "Erdan's sarcasm") are world-specific. Only the generic parts were extracted here.
- F. Some tells conflict with serial pacing conventions (e.g. "end scenes a beat early" and "every scene ends on a setback" vs "open endings"). Reconcile: end on setback or unresolved problem, not on a summary.
- G. Doc says one-sentence paragraphs and short punchy lines are "tells" in density, yet also recommends terse Black Company style; keep for genuine beats.
- H. No guidance on chapter length or cadence for the weekly serial. The doc mentions pirateaba's "serial schedule" only as a source.
- I. Research log is cumulative; later weekly entries may add rules after 2026-11-06.

### A Guide for Heroes (Who'd Rather Not Be)
- Genre/form: comedic isekai / progression fantasy; first person past; a reluctant, sarcastic, paranoid modern gamer is summoned as "the hero".
- Core themes: unwanted destiny and duty versus self-preservation; inheritance of other people's lives and deaths (a hero absorbing the memories and wishes of the fallen, "carrying the wishes of dead men"); whether the system/religion that made you a hero is honest; identity and the id/superego split; surface prejudice vs the reality of those who actually hold back catastrophe (the "villains" do the dirty guard work); found family forming under pressure.
- Character dynamics: a deflating, anxious narrator reacting to bigger, odder personalities; protective quiet women who treat him as a project; bickering adventuring party with loud-vs-plain pairs; politely menacing non-human nobles who are unexpectedly childlike or chaotic; a "familiar"/pact bond where partners are forced into closeness.
- Tone/voice: wry, self-aware, deadpan interior asides in italics/quotes, jokes from reaction not narration; humour beside real danger and bodily gore; fair, rule-bound magic (spell names long to prevent accidental casting) and skills with explicit requirements that are funny in their specificity.
- Recurring motifs: contracts/pacts with a visible mark; foreign mana making the body sick (nausea, headache); chest scar/core warmth; gas/hallucination as memory transfer; a book/manual that explains the world; religion distorted by imported cultural myth; "people can be loud" mundane indifference to chaos; mana as pressure that sinks.

### RULEBREAKERS (+ Lorebook)
- Genre/form: urban/supernatural action, modern-day, third person past.
- Core themes: bodily autonomy and possession (parasitic dead spirits taking hosts); cost and constraint (power always paid for by a tailored drawback, with cleverness in bypassing it); public health and propaganda (a vaccine with a hidden side effect, rumour vs science, trust); loyalty and grief (a hunter trying to recover a possessed friend); the line between predator and companion (taming, eating, bonding); monstrous people who are fun.
- Character dynamics: a pragmatic, scarred, put-upon handler paired with a feral, gleeful "pet" who is also his friend's body; a deadpan driver/logistics woman against a clueless, impulsive partner; a mute, strong, shipping-obsessed teammate; a revenge-driven enemy who returns stronger each meeting; a villain who is a self-sacrificing "patriot" whose power grows with the hatred aimed at him.
- Tone/voice: gallows humour, grounded workplace banter among monster hunters, gore treated casually; absurd power drawbacks (a pose to activate, a kiss as a price, a cop who cannot lie); bureaucracy (credits, reports to HQ, car repair costs).
- Recurring motifs: rule + balance + bypass; red dust when a host body breaks down; pills/vaccine as protection with a hidden cost; eating as transfer of identity; collars/bonds with petty prices (sweat, pats); percentages of "immune" outliers; the world "reset" many times.

### Monolith
- Genre/form: military science fiction / space opera; third person past; squad-level POV with interstitial scenes among distant rulers (and a separate bureaucratic "oversight" antagonist).
- Core themes: war as a century-long stalemate; soldiers as expendable instruments of empires; a prize substance that creates "enhanced" fighters and the horror of becoming one; the tension between obedience and moral breaking points; impersonal bureaucracy that registers, classifies and seizes people; empires fighting an ideological opponent (a "holy" empire vs a technocratic one); judgment, impurity and being "marked".
- Character dynamics: a weary sergeant and green recruits; stoic young officers who do the dangerous thing alone; hunters and hunted flipping; cold administrative officials speaking in registry language versus battered individuals; distant councils discussing a world as a minor tactical move.
- Tone/voice: grim, rain-and-mud atmospheric, terse military procedure, deadpan understatement ("expectations rarely survived the weather"), cold clinical speech for the system characters; chapter titles are single abstract words (Sentence, Pain, Hunger, Effort, Prey, Impurity, Marked).
- Recurring motifs: weather as mood; gas masks and filtered air; halo/particle-orbiting inhuman officials; classification tiers ("High-Tier", "Berserk-Class"); hunger/consumption; marked bodies; premonition; sealed vaults; a ground-level view of a galactic machine.

### In His Shadow
- Genre/form: dark fantasy; first person past; a girl at the bottom of a village (an "errand rat" scorned by the other children) who becomes entangled with a powerful, cold, ambivalent mentor and other dangerous figures.
- Core themes: abuse, social cruelty and survival; trauma surfacing under training; power inherited or imposed (master/familiar, protector/dependent, "She Calls Me Master"); resurrection and its price; faith versus cults (priestesses, a masked cult leader and his broken "Fingers" with abstract names); purity and what it costs; moral ambiguity in empires whose technology (magi-tech) was built through war; trust slowly built between damaged people.
- Character dynamics: a mentor who is cold yet kind, absent yet omnipresent; a soft-spoken but lethal healer-mage who is the group's emotional centre; a proud, offended, stranded devil-princess; a chaotic, flirty, dangerous familiar who attached herself without consent; a zealous but kind priestess; reckless engineers and mad scientists; a cult hierarchy with ritual titles.
- Tone/voice: gritty and intimate; bleak humour at the protagonist's expense; ritual humiliation as "right on schedule"; sudden warmth between violence; atrocity through aftermath; long arcs with "Who's Who" recap pages and layered volume structure.
- Recurring motifs: shadow/light magic; masks; underground tunnels; rat/stray-dog imagery; memory orbs showing god-like battles; named titles of a hierarchy (abstract-noun names); empire lore as prologue (Demon Lord's rise, Dark Ages, magi-tech inventions that appear from a vanishing Catalyst).

### Cross-story synthesis
- Shared themes: (1) power always has a price and the fun is in bypassing it; (2) bodies and identity as contested territory (possession, memory-transfer, resurrection, hosts, familiars); (3) bonds forced by circumstance that become real loyalty; (4) institutions (church, empire, vaccine company, oversight bureaucracy, "system") that mislead or ration truth; (5) "monsters" who are partly the good guys and "heroes" who are partly tools; (6) grief and carrying the dead; (7) war and catastrophe cycles ("Doomsday", resets, century stalemate).
- Dynamics repeated: deadpan or anxious lead plus an eccentric, hungry or feral partner; a quiet competent woman as emotional anchor; bickering teams with comic violence; cold powerful figures with softer inner lives; villains who are ideologically sincere.
- Tone/voice: dry, sardonic, grounded humour beside real gore and loss; rule-bound magic/systems with specific costs; mundane bureaucratic detail inside fantastical settings (credits, registry classifications, errands, spell-name conventions); close, opinionated POV; chapter titles that are punchy phrases or single abstract nouns; heavy use of Who's-Who style lore pages.
- Recurring motifs to echo ABSTRACTLY (not literally): paid-for abilities; consumption/hunger; hosting another's will inside oneself; masks; weather/atmosphere as mood; contracts with visible marks; cults or churches whose theology is wrong; unlikely domestic moments between dangerous people.
