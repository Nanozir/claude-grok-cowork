# Chapter 1: A Stick That Splits

Teele Rõuk got one cut, the lowest result the machine ever gave anyone, and she cheered as if she had won the harvest lottery.

She stood in the middle of the Cutting Hall with both fists in the air, one of them holding her hazel stick, while Reckoner Lind rang the little bell on his desk for order. The hall was cold and smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, and the sixteen-year-olds still waiting along the wall, a few dozen of them, could not decide whether to laugh. One of them, Aarne Kallas, was next in line and hoped for three cuts, enough for a clerk's desk at the Notchery. Teele put two fingers in her mouth and whistled, two notes, the second lower and rather rude. A grey rat came out from under the registration desk, sat up, and looked at her.

"There," said Teele. "That's my skill. Nobody conscripts a rat-whistler. They can't even draft me to carry things. Ask anyone."

"Move along, Miss Rõuk," said Lind. "The next candidate is waiting."

"I'm told he's very nice."

"Along."

Aarne thought it was the most sensible thing anyone had said all morning.

Every citizen of Orrova came to this hall in the winter they turned sixteen and pushed a hand-length rod of hazel into the Notchery's machine. The machine was a bronze frame twice the height of a man, with a slot in the front, and nobody had ever found the crank that turned it. It cut notches in the rod, anywhere from one to seven, and it gave each person one skill to go with them. The number of cuts was how strong the skill was. A one-cut skill was a whistle for rats, and a seven-cut skill moved armies, and the Notchery, which had run the machine for four hundred years, licensed you to a trade by the count. One cut meant hauling, sweeping or mending nets. Three meant a clerk, a boatwright or a midwife. Seven meant the Notchery sent a coach for you, and Aarne had never met anyone who had been sent for.

The Notchery could tell you everything about the machine except why it worked. What it did know was who lied about their cuts, because afterwards a clerk split every stick lengthwise with a chisel and filed one half in the cellar, and the two halves had to match. The citizen kept the other half to show landlords and be buried with.

Three cuts would make him a clerk at the Notchery, like his father before he died two winters ago, with a wage, a stove, and a reason for his sister Liis to stay in school past her tenth birthday. For two years the annex had let him copy forms in the evenings to pay off the funeral. He had been weighed already on the iron scale by the door, because the Notchery wrote down everything it could get hold of, and the register said *Kallas, A.: 52,140 grams*, which his mother called thin.

Outside, the bell on the evaporation pans rang twice. That meant a rime had been seen out on the salt flats, one of the crusted salt figures that sometimes walked in off the pans, and nobody in the hall looked up, because the pan bell rang twice most winters.

"Next," said Lind. He was a narrow man with ink down the side of one hand, and he read names as if each one was an invoice. "Kallas, Aarne."

The slot was warm. Aarne pushed the rod in until the wheel behind it took hold, and felt it go on taking: the machine cut, and cut again, past three, four, five, six, and he said out loud, "That's too many, I only wanted three." It went past seven, which was the top of the ladder and the end of the usable rod. Then something cracked, with a long dry sound like a knuckle the size of a barn, and the machine stopped.

The stick came out in his hand, broken lengthwise down the whole of its length. He was holding one half. The other half had gone back into the slot and was not there any more. He waited to feel a skill arrive, the way Teele said hers had, like a coin dropped into a pocket, and nothing arrived. The boy in the leather apron who did the chiselling stood with his chisel raised over a stick that had already been split for him, and looked at Lind, and Lind did not look at him.

"That is a Split," Lind said. He didn't say it loudly. "A stick that breaks by itself, inside the machine, and the machine keeps the other half. There has not been one in sixty-one years."

Somebody at the back screamed. Somebody laughed, and then the whole hall was talking at once, and Teele was shouting across the hall, "Aarne Kallas, I will be your friend until I die!"

"You are already my friend," said Aarne. "You owe me for nine pies."

"That's just friendship with paperwork!"

He looked down, and that was when the stick began to write.

It happened on the pale flat where it had broken: pencil, grey, a clerk's small upright hand, one line at a time, as if somebody was taking dictation from nobody. It looked like his father's hand, or like the hand every Notchery clerk was taught, which was the same thing until you looked at the tails of the g's.

> *Account opened: Kallas, Aarne.*
> *You have no skill of your own. You may borrow any skill you have watched someone use within the past hour, and use it at the highest strength there is.*
> *The price is taken from your body.*
> *The account must be settled by the end of the seventh day.*

He read it twice, because it was a document you read twice. Then, because the annex had taught him to find the missing piece in any odd paper before reading the rest, he looked for it. A stick that split made one half to keep and one half to file. He had the half to keep. The Notchery had no half to file, because the machine had swallowed it, and somebody, somewhere, held the other half of his stick.

Borrowing a skill sounded like a gift until you asked who had to be asked, and what *taken from the body* was supposed to mean, and why a document that sounded so polite had not put a number on it. He had read enough loan agreements to know that the sentence without a number was the one that cost money.

He rubbed a line with his thumb. It smudged, and underneath the same line was written again in clean graphite.

"Master Kallas." Lind had come down off his dais. He did not offer congratulations. He took Aarne by the sleeve and steered him behind the registration screen, where there was a second desk, a stove nobody had lit, and a rack of numbered drawers. "A word. Sit."

Aarne sat. Lind did not.

"There is an ordinance," Lind said. "Number fourteen. A Split is held, which we call Pending, until a registered household lodges a surety of four hundred kroons with the Notchery. A surety is a promise to pay if the account fails. If nobody lodges one by the end of the seventh day, the Notchery collects on the account."

"Collects what?"

"The cost of the cutting, from your family. Four hundred kroons."

"That's the same number."

"It is a well-drafted ordinance."

Aarne's mother made about fifteen kroons a month on a good month, selling cabbage-and-egg pies from a cart under the Salt Gate, and had never lodged anything with anybody. "And the stick says the price is taken from my body. What happens to the body if the account fails?"

Lind looked at the numbered drawers. He looked at them for what Aarne would have called a second, and Aarne counted it as more. "The register for the last Split says *forfeit*," he said. "It does not say what was done with it."

"The last Split. Sixty-one years ago."

"Yes."

"Why seven days?"

"It is what the ordinance says," said Lind, with the tone of a man who had checked.

Aarne turned his father's pencil stub over in his fingers. It was a Notchery pencil, stamped on the side, worn to the width of a thumb. His father had kept the annex shelves for nineteen years and had said a lot of things about forms, most of them dull. One of them had stuck.

"I'd like the Pending in writing," he said. "With the date, your seal and a number."

"Is that necessary?"

"My father used to say a stamp without a receipt is a rumour."

Lind's pen stopped over the paper. "Your father was Arvo Kallas."

"Yes."

"He kept the annex."

"He did."

"Hm," said Lind. He took a receipt book from the drawer, wrote, blotted, sealed, tore along the dotted edge and held it out. *No. 4417. Split stick, Kallas A., held Pending under Ordinance 14 until the end of the seventh day, to be altered by no one but a bench.* Then he heated a brand at the stove's single coal and burned PENDING into the bark of the stick.

"Seven days, Master Kallas," he said. "I would eat well."

---

His mother's cart stood where it had stood for ten years, under the arch of the Salt Gate, with a tin lid propped over it against the sleet and a brazier underneath. Liis sat on an upturned pie crate doing sums with a charcoal end. She looked up when he came through the crowd, at the broken stick with PENDING burned into it, and then at his face.

"Well?" said Hilja Kallas. She was turning pies with a flat knife and didn't stop.

"It split," said Aarne. "Right down the middle. It's higher than seven. There isn't a higher, so they hold it until a household vouches for it. Four hundred kroons, within a week."

His mother put a pie on a square of paper. She did it with great care, as if it needed her attention. "Do they pay for it if we can't?"

"No. We pay."

"Then we won't be able to," said Hilja. "So we will have to find a way." She pushed the paper into his hand. "Eat. You've gone thin."

"Do you get a sword?" said Liis.

"I get a receipt."

"Can I see it?"

He gave it to her. She read it with her lips moving, got as far as *held Pending*, and looked at him with the grave respect of a nine-year-old who has been told that paper is important. "It's got a number," she said.

"Four-four-one-seven. Don't fold it."

Teele had caught up with him at the Gate, sleet-soaked and loud, and tapped his broken stick for luck before her aunt's pawnshop swallowed her back. "Come round tonight," she said. "I read contracts for a living. Don't sign anything with a man who's smiling." He told her he hadn't signed anything. "Yet," said Teele.

He stood at the edge of the brazier's warmth and ate, as he had at every school lunch of his life. The pie was hot on the outside and cold in the middle, because his mother had never in ten years worked out her brazier, and he would not have changed that. A few paces off a Notchery runner was already at the Gate sergeant's elbow, and then the fishmonger's, and the news was going down the street a little improved at each stall. His mother went on folding dough beside him and had not yet asked what the stick said, and he had kept it under his coat in his other hand and had not looked.

When he did look, it had written something new, below the rest, in the same small hand.
