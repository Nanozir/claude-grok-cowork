# Chapter 2: The Price of a Whistle

Aunt Reet's pawnshop had a front room for customers and a back room for the truth. The front room held forty years of other people's good intentions on hooks: wedding spoons, a barber's chair, a stuffed pike with a ticket tied to its tail. The back room held a counter, two stools, a lamp, and the best scale in the Salt Quarter, a brass balance with sliding weights that Reet used to catch customers who had put a lead washer inside a silver ring.

"Fifty-two thousand one hundred and forty," Teele said, reading the beam. She had made him stand on the flat pan in his stockings with his coat off. "That is you. Say it back."

"Fifty-two thousand one hundred and forty grams."

"Good. Keep still. Aunt Reet will charge me for wear."

"I am keeping still."

"You're doing something with your eyebrows."

"I'm reading," said Aarne. The broken stick lay on the counter beside the lamp with the receipt tucked under it. He had read the whole text of it to her already, from *Account opened* to the empty line after *Creditor*, and Teele had listened with a dried plum in her cheek, which she moved from one side to the other at the interesting parts. It had crossed about nine times.

"Right," she said. "I'm not going to explain a surety to a clerk's son. Here's something you don't know, because it's not in the forms. When a surety's promise gets called in, the collector goes to the signer who has the least to lose and the most to be ashamed of, and squeezes that one first. So whoever you get to sign for you, find out who else is on the page with them."

"That's useful."

"It's the only thing I know that's useful. The rest is guesswork." She spat the plum stone into a dish. "The stick says you pay with your body. How much?"

"It doesn't say."

"Then you find out before you need to." Teele hopped up on the counter and swung her boots. "You can borrow skills. You said so yourself. Any skill you've seen someone use in the last hour. Well. I'm somebody. I've got a skill."

"A whistle."

"A good one."

She whistled the two notes again, short, and in the front room something went *tik-tik-tik* across the boards. The grey rat from the Cutting Hall, which had followed her home, sat up in the doorway and watched her. It was the one thing the machine had found worth cutting into Teele Rõuk, though she had practised the two notes since she was six, and no rat had ever answered them until the machine gave her the skill this morning.

"There. Observed," said Teele. "Take it easy on the poor thing."

Aarne's stick had already begun to write.

> *Loan offered: Rat-Whistle, one cut, lent by Rõuk, Teele.*
> *At your strength of eight cuts, it costs one hundred and seventy-five grams of your body for every second it is doing something. (Twenty-five grams for each cut it is raised, and it is raised seven.)*
> *While you hold a skill, nothing is taken. While the loan stands, the lender cannot use the skill.*
> *Loan ends when returned.*

"It's a hundred and seventy-five a second," he said. "Because yours is the lowest there is and I'd be running it at the top, so it has to climb seven cuts, and each cut costs twenty-five. It only charges while the whistle is happening. And it moves, it doesn't copy. You'd lose it while I have it."

"I'd lose my whistle?"

"Until I give it back."

Teele put a hand on her chest and staggered, which was a poor performance of a woman being stabbed. "Aarne. I have whistled since I was six. It is my only talent."

"I can leave it."

"I'm being dramatic, it's a rat whistle, I've had it since this morning and used it twice, both times on purpose." She pointed the plum stone at him. "But it comes back in one piece."

"Six seconds, and I stop."

"How much is that?"

"A little over a kilo. A litre of water." He had worked it out on the back of the receipt book while she talked. A second line had gone onto the stick under the first, small and grey. *Floor: 40,000 grams. A loan that would take you below it is refused.* "I can't go lower than forty kilos. That leaves about twelve to spend in total."

"And they come back when you eat?"

"About a hundred grams a day, if my mother wins."

Teele looked at him a while, and then at the stick, and her grin got smaller. "Right," she said. "Six seconds. I'll hold the watch."

She had one on a string, a tin one with a single hand. He accepted the loan, and nothing happened except for a small settled weight in his chest, as if a figure had been entered somewhere in him without anyone mentioning it. *Loan accepted*, wrote the stick, and under it a running figure, which began at nothing. He put his first two fingers against his lip, the way she had, and whistled.

What came out was the same two notes, but they did not seem to come from his mouth. They came from the room. The brass scale chimed on its beam. The lamp flame lay down flat. The grey rat in the doorway stood up on its hind legs, and under the floor and inside the walls and from the cellar and out of a tin of buttons on the counter, something began to run.

"Three," said Teele, very high. "Four."

They came under the door in a rush. A fat brown one climbed the leg of the stool and sat in Teele's lap, and she lifted her hands and said "Five" in a voice that did not match her face. In the front room a customer shouted, and another one, and a chair went over.

"Six."

He stopped. The sound went out of the room, and the rats did not leave. They sat on the floor in rows, hundreds of them, with their faces turned up.

His knees had gone, and the room had got cold in the way a room does when you stand up too fast. Aarne sat on the stool. His trousers had slipped down on his hips, and he found that he was very hungry for something sweet, and took a plum, and the plum was gritty and he ate all of it.

"Weigh me."

Teele put the rat down, climbed onto the counter again and read the beam. "Fifty-one thousand and ninety." She looked up. "A thousand and fifty. To the gram."

On the stick, in its small hand: *Interest this loan: 1,050. Body: 51,090.* And below it, *To return the skill, say* return.

"Return," said Aarne, and felt it go out of him, the same small weight lifting away.

"Oh," said Teele, and whistled, and every rat in the room turned its head.

"Don't!"

"Sorry!" She stopped. "But it's mine again, look, it's all mine." She tried a second time, shorter, and a rat the size of a loaf rolled over on its back. She laughed so hard she had to put her forehead down on the counter.

"I paid a litre of myself for that."

"And I'm sure it was well spent." She wiped an eye. "No, listen. That was a one-cut skill, the cheapest there is. Look at them. There are rats in this room I have never seen. Those are from the *canal*. They've been leaving the flats since the pan bell started ringing. Aunt Reet says rats go inland a week before a rime walks, and she won't open the shop after dark until it's over."

"What does a rime do, when it walks?"

"Comes in off the salt flats toward the lamps and takes somebody who's behind on something. It's all in the warning pamphlet, which nobody has read." She shrugged. "The pan guards salt it back down. They always have."

Aarne looked. They sat in the edge of the lamplight, huge and wet, with their scarred ears forward. "The price depends on the gap," he said slowly. "A hundred and seventy-five a second, because you are a one. If I borrowed from somebody who was a six, it would be fifty."

"So borrow from a six."

"I'd have to be watching a six use it, inside the hour. And they'd have to do without it for as long as I held it."

"That's very specific," said Teele. "Who thought of that?"

"Not me."

At the back of the crowd one rat had not sat down. It was an old grey animal with a bitten ear and a limp, and it came across the boards through the others like someone walking in to a rent hearing. The others had come because they were called. This one had brought something. At Aarne's boot it stopped, dropped it out of its jaws, and sat back on its haunches.

"Rats hoard shiny things," Teele said, bending. "Brass, mostly. Spoons. Once, a bishop's ring."

It wasn't brass. It was half a stick, grey and soft with age and furred green along one edge from some cellar where it had spent a long time getting damp. It had been broken lengthwise, down the whole of its length. On the flat face, under a crust of dirt, a name had been cut with a knife in small upright letters.

REBANE, E.

Aarne did not pick it up. The stick on the counter had started writing before he could look away.

> *Previous Split: Rebane, E. Opened sixty-one years ago.*
> *Closed in default on the seventh day.*
> *Body: forfeit.*

"What does it say?" said Teele. "Your face is doing the thing."

"That somebody else was handed this exact page first. And that he didn't settle." He told her the rest, and watched her stop chewing. "Have you heard the name?"

"Never. Hold on." She went into the front room, stepping through rats, and came back with the pledge book, which went back seventy years to Reet's grandfather, and turned pages with a wet thumb while the rats watched her. She counted back sixty-one winters and stopped. "Here. Somebody has cut the leaves out of that year with a razor, right down at the spine. And not a Rebane anywhere else in the book, not an E. anything. Aunt Reet takes in half the Quarter's spoons, and not one of them ever had that name."

"Then nobody remembers him."

"Or somebody made sure." She shut the book.

The lamp went on burning. Through the doorway the rats were leaving in the order they had come, and the stuffed pike with its ticket stayed where it was in the front room, looking past all of them at the street.

He picked the old half up with the end of his pencil, the way you pick up something you aren't sure is dead, and laid it on the counter beside his own. They were the same length. Each was cut with seven notches along its edge, and then an eighth cut across the break where the machine had no business making one.
